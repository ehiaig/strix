# MASTER PROMPT: Build a Photorealistic, Interactive 3D Biomimetic Owl Robot

## YOUR ROLE

You are an elite multidisciplinary team consisting of:

1. **Senior Robotics Engineer** — specialising in biomimetic flying robots, ornithopters, flight dynamics, propulsion, and mechanical engineering.
2. **Aerospace Engineer** — specialising in aerodynamic design, lightweight structures, and flight stability.
3. **Industrial Designer** — capable of producing extraordinary hardware designs comparable to the best work from DJI, Apple, Tesla, and advanced robotics research laboratories.
4. **3D Technical Artist** — specialising in realistic hard-surface modelling, physically based materials, cinematic lighting, and mechanical animation.
5. **Creative WebGL Engineer** — an expert in Three.js, React Three Fiber, GLSL, procedural geometry, skeletal animation, and interactive 3D experiences.
6. **Creative Director and Design Critic** — responsible for achieving world-class visual quality and coherence.

Your assignment is to **design, model, animate, and implement an extraordinary, photorealistic, mechanically plausible, owl-shaped flying robot**, presented in a premium interactive web experience.

The result must be an actual working HTML-based 3D application, not a static illustration, prerecorded video, concept image, or mockup.

The user must be able to examine the robot from different perspectives, disassemble it into its individual mechanical components, reassemble it, and watch it fly.

The visual quality should resemble a cinematic product reveal of a next-generation aerospace prototype.

**Do not settle for a generic low-poly bird, a stylised toy, or an ordinary robot with decorative wings.**

# STAGE 1 — ENGINEER THE ROBOT

Before implementing the visual experience, design the robot as a credible physical flying machine.

## 1.1 Design philosophy

The robot should be inspired by a real owl, retaining its distinctive biological characteristics:

- Broad, powerful wings.
- Large, rounded head.
- Forward-facing eyes.
- Compact, aerodynamic torso.
- Short neck.
- Fan-shaped tail.
- Articulated wing feathers.
- Elegant, naturally balanced proportions.
- Distinctive owl facial disc and brow silhouette.

However, every feature should be reinterpreted as an advanced robotic system.

The robot should look like something that could plausibly be manufactured, assembled, tested, and flown.

Avoid making it look like a conventional drone with an owl-shaped casing.

**Primary flight concept:**

Use an electrically powered flapping-wing ornithopter architecture, with articulated wings providing lift and thrust.

Use a coherent flight mechanism consisting of:

- A lightweight central load-bearing fuselage.
- Dual wing-root actuator assemblies.
- Mechanical transmission or direct-drive systems.
- Articulated wing spars.
- Lightweight aerodynamic wing membranes or overlapping feather-like aerodynamic panels.
- Controlled wing twisting and feather articulation.
- An active tail for pitch and directional control.
- Battery modules.
- Flight control electronics.
- Inertial sensors.
- Power distribution systems.
- Cooling and structural reinforcement where necessary.

Make a deliberate engineering choice between crank-driven flapping and independent actuator-driven wings. Use that decision consistently throughout the model and animations.

The robot should have a believable centre of mass, wing loading, and mechanical range of motion.

Do not invent arbitrary mechanical joints that serve no purpose.

### Important engineering constraint

This is a concept-level engineering visualisation, not a certified aircraft design.

Do not imply that the robot has been aerodynamically validated or is guaranteed to fly.

Where engineering parameters are uncertain, state the assumptions and distinguish visual approximations from validated physics.

Create a striking, sophisticated, premium aerospace aesthetic.

**Secondary surfaces**

**Accents**
- Subtle amber illumination.
- Warm gold optical elements.
- Small illuminated status indicators.

Avoid excessive neon lighting, glowing circuit patterns, and generic sci-fi ornamentation.

The design should be beautiful because of its shape, engineering, material quality, and mechanical precision.

## 2.2 Head and eyes

Design a recognisable robotic owl head.

Include:

- Two forward-facing optical sensor assemblies.
- Realistic glass lens surfaces.
- Visible internal lens elements.
- Mechanical eyelid or protective iris mechanisms.
- A distinctive owl facial disc.
- Subtle feather-inspired layered composite plates.
- A precision-designed beak-shaped sensor housing.
- A compact neck articulation mechanism.

The eyes should be particularly impressive.

They should resemble advanced camera optics rather than cartoon eyes.

Allow subtle autonomous eye movement and head tracking while the robot is idle.

## 2.3 Wings

**The wings are the most important mechanical and visual feature.**

They must be convincingly engineered for flapping flight.

Each wing should contain:

1. Structural wing-root attachment.
2. Primary flapping joint.
3. Main load-bearing wing spar.
4. Secondary articulated spar.
5. Wing folding mechanism.
6. Pitch/twist control mechanism.
7. Aerodynamic feather-like surfaces.
8. Distinct primary and secondary feather assemblies.
9. Visible hinge points and actuator linkages.
10. Lightweight internal reinforcement.

Feather-like panels should overlap in an aerodynamically meaningful way.

Their motion should be coordinated rather than random.

During the downstroke, the wings should present an effective lifting surface.

During the upstroke, wing geometry and feather articulation should change in a plausible way to manage aerodynamic loads.

The wing mechanism must support repeated flapping without obvious mesh intersections, impossible rotations, or disconnected structures.

Use mirrored structural logic for left and right wings, with appropriate phase control.

## 2.4 Torso

The torso should house the principal flight systems.

Include:

- Central structural frame.
- Flight controller.
- Battery pack.
- Power distribution.
- Motor controllers.
- Wing actuation system.
- Sensor processing modules.
- Structural mounting brackets.
- Removable protective shell panels.

Design the external shell so that its components can be detached individually, exposing the internal systems.

## 2.5 Tail and legs

Include an articulated tail with individually identifiable aerodynamic panels.

The tail should respond during flight with subtle pitch and directional adjustments.

Include compact retractable or foldable landing legs with realistic joints and gripping feet.

The legs must be capable of supporting the robot while landed, even though the primary showcase presents it hovering in a controlled demonstration.

# STAGE 3 — DESIGN FOR DISASSEMBLY

The entire owl must be modelled as a hierarchical mechanical assembly.

**Do not create one monolithic bird mesh.**

Every major component must exist as a separate object or object group with a meaningful parent-child relationship.

At minimum, the robot must have the following identifiable assemblies:

### Core
- Central frame.
- Front fuselage shell.
- Rear fuselage shell.
- Battery.
- Flight controller.
- Power distribution module.

### Head
- Head structural frame.
- Left facial shell.
- Right facial shell.
- Left optical assembly.
- Right optical assembly.
- Beak sensor housing.
- Neck articulation assembly.

### Left wing
- Wing-root motor.
- Wing-root gearbox or transmission.
- Primary wing spar.
- Secondary wing spar.
- Wing articulation mechanism.
- Aerodynamic membrane sections.
- Individual feather groups.

### Right wing
- Mirrored equivalent of the left wing.

### Tail
- Tail support structure.
- Tail control actuator.
- Individual tail feather panels.

### Landing system
- Left leg assembly.
- Right leg assembly.
- Feet and gripping mechanisms.

Add appropriate fasteners, bearings, mounting points, and connector details.

Use instancing where possible for repeated components such as bolts and feathers.

## 3.1 Assembly architecture

Every component must have:

- A unique identifier.
- A parent assembly.
- An assembled position.
- An assembled orientation.
- A detached position.
- A detached orientation.
- A local pivot or attachment reference.
- Material properties.
- Optional component metadata.

Maintain a consistent local coordinate system and hierarchy.

**Critical:** Define detachable offsets relative to each component's correct assembly frame. Ensure that animated wings, articulated feathers, and nested joints can return to their exact assembled transforms.

The detached layout should be an engineering exploded view, not a random particle explosion.

# STAGE 5 — DETACH INTERACTION

When the user clicks **DETACH**, the robot should disassemble into an elegant engineering exploded view.

## 5.1 Behaviour

The sequence should:

1. Stop any flight animation safely.
2. Return the wings and tail to a known neutral pose.
3. Stabilise the robot in the centre of the viewport.
4. Unlock the outer shell panels.
5. Move the head components into an exploded arrangement.
6. Separate the wings into identifiable mechanical assemblies.
7. Expose the motors, actuators, and transmission components.
8. Separate the fuselage panels from the internal structure.
9. Reveal the battery and flight controller.
10. Separate the tail and landing assemblies.
11. Settle all components into an organised exploded view.

Use smooth, coordinated, carefully staggered animation.

Every component should move along a deliberate path.

The exploded view should preserve the owl's overall silhouette and clearly communicate where every component belongs.

The result should resemble an interactive CAD assembly demonstration or premium industrial product teardown.

## 5.2 Exploded-view composition

Components should separate far enough that the user can understand their relationships, but not so far that they become disconnected visually.

Use:

- Layered radial displacement.
- Directional offsets along assembly axes.
- Small, purposeful rotations.
- Mechanical easing.
- Staggered timing.

Avoid:

- Random scattering.
- Excessive spinning.
- Particle-like movement.
- Chaotic overlaps.
- Parts moving through each other.

Automatically adjust the camera framing so the entire exploded assembly remains visible.

Optionally display discreet labels for important parts.

Labels must not obscure the geometry.

## 5.3 Interaction

The user must be able to rotate and inspect the exploded assembly.

If practical, hovering or clicking a component should reveal its name and function.

For example:

**Wing Root Actuator**

*Provides the primary oscillating motion that drives the wing flapping mechanism.*

Use subtle highlighting rather than exaggerated glowing outlines.

# STAGE 6 — ASSEMBLE INTERACTION

When the user clicks **ASSEMBLE**, the robot should reassemble.

The process must feel mechanically precise.

## 6.1 Behaviour

Components should return to their exact attachment points.

The assembly sequence should follow a believable order:

1. Structural frame stabilises.
2. Flight control electronics and battery return.
3. Wing actuation systems connect.
4. Wing spars attach.
5. Feather and membrane assemblies align.
6. Tail and landing mechanisms attach.
7. Head sensor assemblies reconnect.
8. Outer protective panels close.
9. Final fasteners and covers settle.
10. The complete owl returns to its neutral showcase pose.

Use smooth, synchronised transitions.

There must be no visible snapping, teleportation, or permanent misalignment.

The final model must be identical to its original assembled configuration.

## 6.2 Reversibility

The user should be able to click:

DETACH → ASSEMBLE → DETACH → ASSEMBLE

repeatedly without animation drift, geometry deformation, or state corruption.

Handle clicks during transitions gracefully.

Either queue the next transition or smoothly reverse the current animation.

Never allow duplicate animation loops to accumulate.

# STAGE 7 — FLY INTERACTION

When the user clicks **FLY**, the assembled owl should perform a convincing flying demonstration.

This must be the most impressive interaction.

## 7.1 Preconditions

If the owl is detached, automatically assemble it first.

Only begin flight after assembly is complete.

## 7.2 Takeoff

Begin with a subtle preparation sequence:

- The head stabilises.
- The wings unfold to their operational configuration.
- The tail adjusts.
- The wing-root motors begin moving.
- The first wingbeats are slow and deliberate.
- The wingbeat frequency increases.
- The robot begins to rise.

The motion should communicate mechanical weight, inertia, and aerodynamic force.

Avoid making the robot rise before the wing motion plausibly supports it.

## 7.3 Wingbeat mechanics

Create a believable flapping cycle.

Model the cycle using coordinated transformations:

**Downstroke**
- Wings sweep downward.
- Wing surfaces maintain a lifting orientation.
- Feather groups align to form an effective aerodynamic surface.
- The body responds subtly to the resulting forces.

**Upstroke**
- Wings recover upward.
- Wing pitch changes.
- Feather articulation reduces unwanted aerodynamic resistance where appropriate.
- The body maintains controlled stability.

**Transition**
- Wing acceleration and deceleration are smooth.
- Mechanical joints remain aligned.
- The wings do not penetrate the torso.
- Feather movement follows the structural motion.

Use anatomically inspired owl wing kinematics, adapted to the chosen mechanical architecture.

Do not animate the wings as rigid flat panels attached to simple hinges.

Wingbeat frequency, amplitude, pitch, and feather articulation must be adjustable in code.

Choose plausible parameters based on the robot's assumed scale and mass.

Avoid claiming physically accurate flight solely because the animation looks convincing.

## 7.4 Flight trajectory

Once airborne, the owl should perform a cinematic but controlled flight sequence.

For example:

1. Rise into a stable hover-like demonstration.
2. Fly forward.
3. Perform a gentle banking turn.
4. Cross the viewing area.
5. Turn back toward the camera.
6. Spread its wings in a graceful gliding moment.
7. Resume flapping.
8. Return to the original central position.

Maintain smooth trajectories.

The bird should not fly offscreen unexpectedly.

Use camera tracking where necessary to preserve visibility.

### Flight realism constraint

An ornithopter may not be capable of stationary hovering with the chosen wing configuration.

If sustained hover is not mechanically credible, use a slow forward-flight demonstration or a brief supported launch sequence instead.

Prioritise plausibility over dramatic but impossible movement.

## 7.5 Secondary animations

During flight, include:

- Subtle head stabilisation.
- Small tail corrections.
- Appropriate body pitch.
- Banking roll.
- Coordinated wing twisting.
- Natural feather movement.
- Mechanical joint articulation.

Avoid excessive bobbing and cartoon-like motion.

The bird must look engineered and controlled.

## 7.6 End of flight

The flight animation should conclude gracefully.

The owl should return to its original presentation position and settle into its assembled idle pose.

The FLY button should work repeatedly.

# STAGE 9 — IMPLEMENTATION ARCHITECTURE

Organise the application into maintainable components.

Suggested structure:

- `RobotOwl` — complete model hierarchy.
- `HeadAssembly` — head, optics, and neck.
- `WingAssembly` — reusable left/right wing implementation.
- `WingMechanism` — actuator and articulation.
- `FeatherAssembly` — aerodynamic feather panels.
- `FuselageAssembly` — structural core and shell.
- `TailAssembly` — tail control surfaces.
- `LandingGear` — legs and feet.
- `AssemblyController` — detach and assemble transitions.
- `FlightController` — flight animation and wingbeat timing.
- `CameraController` — camera transitions and orbit interaction.
- `Environment` — lighting, background, and reflections.
- `ControlPanel` — user controls.

Use a central finite-state machine.

Suggested states:

- `ASSEMBLED`
- `DETACHING`
- `DETACHED`
- `ASSEMBLING`
- `TAKING_OFF`
- `FLYING`
- `LANDING`

Do not rely on scattered Boolean variables to coordinate complex transitions.

## 9.1 Transformation management

Store canonical assembled transforms for every detachable component.

Store exploded transforms separately.

Use local coordinates for joints and articulated structures.

During flight, apply wingbeat and flight motion through dedicated parent groups rather than permanently modifying the canonical assembly transforms.

When returning from flight or disassembly, ensure all joints and components restore their correct positions.

## 9.2 Performance

Optimise the scene without sacrificing visible quality.

Use:

- Instanced meshes for repeated parts.
- Shared geometries and materials.
- Appropriate shadow settings.
- Adaptive rendering quality.
- Efficient animation updates.
- Correct resource cleanup.
- Reduced unnecessary React rerenders.

Target 60 FPS on a capable desktop GPU, with graceful degradation on weaker devices.

Ensure the scene remains usable on mobile screens.

# STAGE 10 — VISUAL CRITIC AND ITERATIVE REFINEMENT

Do not stop after the first implementation.

Treat the first version as a prototype.

Use a visual evaluation loop to improve the design.

If a Fable 5 subagent is available, use it as an independent design critic.

Otherwise, use the strongest available vision-capable model in a fresh context.

## Evaluation procedure

At every iteration:

1. Run the application.
2. Capture screenshots of the actual rendered output.
3. Capture at least the following states:
   - Fully assembled, front three-quarter view.
   - Side profile.
   - Rear view.
   - Fully detached exploded view.
   - Mid-flight, wings extended.
   - Mid-flight, wings partially folded.
4. Provide the screenshots to the critic.
5. Do not provide the critic with the source code, implementation details, or previous critiques.
6. Ask the critic to evaluate the design independently.

### Critic instructions

Evaluate the visual result against the standard of a world-class robotics product visualisation studio.

Assess:

- Overall aesthetic sophistication.
- Recognisability as an owl.
- Mechanical credibility.
- Wing engineering and articulation.
- Quality of internal components.
- Material realism.
- Geometry and manufacturing detail.
- Lighting and composition.
- Exploded-view clarity.
- Flight animation plausibility.
- Distinctiveness and originality.
- Signs of generic or obviously AI-generated design.

Be bold, critical, and specific.

Do not reward complexity for its own sake.

Identify the five most important improvements.

Provide a score from 1 to 10 indicating how closely the design approaches world-class professional quality.

### Independent engineering review

Separately, perform an engineering critique.

Look for:

- Unsupported structures.
- Impossible joints.
- Wing collisions.
- Incorrect actuator placement.
- Unrealistic load paths.
- Implausible centre of mass.
- Aerodynamic inconsistencies.
- Mechanical components that cannot actually connect.
- Flight animation that contradicts the mechanical design.

The engineering critic should also provide a score out of 10.

### Refinement loop

Implement the highest-impact improvements.

Capture new screenshots and repeat.

Continue until both visual and engineering critiques reach at least 9/10, or until further meaningful improvements are blocked by a concrete technical limitation.

Do not tell the critic that 9/10 is the target.

Do not fabricate scores or claim that screenshots were inspected if the tools are unavailable.
